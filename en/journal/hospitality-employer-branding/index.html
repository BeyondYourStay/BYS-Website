Hospitality Employer Branding: Show People What Working With You Actually Feels Like

Sep 9, 2026 · 9 min read · By Yoran Ruis

Hospitality businesses are very good at showing people what it feels like to visit.

The light through a hotel window. The kitchen just before service. The first drink arriving at a table. The quiet of a treatment room. The energy before the gates of an attraction open.

Then a vacancy goes live.

And suddenly the same business becomes:

"Competitive salary. Friendly team. Dynamic environment. Apply now."

The place still has a story.

The people working there still experience it every day.

But almost none of that makes it into the recruitment.

That is the space hospitality employer branding should fill.

Your employer brand exists before someone starts job hunting

Recruitment and employer branding overlap, but they are not the same thing.

Recruitment has an immediate vacancy to fill.

Employer branding shapes what people understand and expect about you as an employer, including when there is no vacancy open.

Hospitality research has treated employer branding as a longer-term strategy connected not only to attraction, but also to employee engagement and retention. One of the recurring mistakes identified in earlier hospitality research was treating employer branding as little more than making job advertisements look better.

That distinction matters because a candidate rarely evaluates a job in isolation.

They evaluate the world around it.

Who will I work with?

What does a normal shift feel like?

How are people managed?

Will I learn anything here?

What is expected when it gets busy?

What kind of person seems to enjoy working here?

A salary figure, job title and list of responsibilities can answer part of the decision.

They cannot answer all of it.

And in hospitality, the employment experience matters particularly because employees are not hidden behind the product. They are part of how the experience is delivered to the guest. Research in tourism and hospitality has repeatedly connected employer attractiveness with factors such as working conditions, development, organisational reputation, economic value and the employee's experience of the workplace itself.

So the employer brand cannot be separated completely from the real place.

It has to come from it.

Start with the candidate, not the content calendar

The Compass was built for hospitality marketing around one principle:

understand first, act second.

The customer is the person moving through the story. The business becomes the guide.

For employer branding, the audience changes.

The logic does not.

Instead of asking:

What recruitment content should we post?

Start with:

Who are we trying to move?

A first hospitality job and a senior kitchen position are not the same decision.

Neither are a seasonal role at a holiday park, a spa therapist position, a restaurant manager vacancy and a hotel reception job.

Age and job title give you part of the picture.

Motivation gives you more.

Someone may be looking for flexibility around university.

Another wants stability after years of changing employers.

Someone else wants a kitchen where they can develop technically.

An experienced hotel professional may be looking for more ownership rather than another job with the same title.

Before creating recruitment content, understand what that person wants, what they are tired of, what makes them hesitate and what they need to see before they believe your workplace could suit them.

Employer branding becomes much more useful when it answers those questions.

Do not manufacture culture. Make the real culture visible

Hospitality marketing often fails when the marketing promise and the guest experience are different.

Employer branding has the same weakness.

If recruitment content presents every shift as relaxed, social and effortless while the reality is intense service, irregular hours and high expectations, stronger marketing has not solved the problem.

It has made the expectation gap larger.

The aim should not be to make work look perfect.

It should be to make the right parts of the work clear.

A busy open kitchen can be demanding and still be attractive to a chef who wants pace, standards and responsibility.

A small boutique hotel may offer less formal hierarchy, but more direct ownership of the guest experience.

A seasonal leisure business may have peaks that do not suit everyone, but be exactly what someone wants for a summer role.

A wellness retreat might offer a quieter rhythm and deeper one-to-one guest interaction.

Good employer branding has edges.

It helps some candidates recognise themselves.

And it helps others decide that the role is not for them.

Both outcomes can be useful.

Turn employment features into moments people can understand

Hospitality brands often describe themselves to candidates with abstract claims.

"Great culture."

"Room to grow."

"Close team."

"Flexible employer."

Those ideas become more believable when they are attached to something visible.

Inside The Compass, we often move from:

Feature → Moment → Feeling → Desire.

The same thinking can be applied to employee experience.

Take:

Feature: Team meal before service.

That becomes:

Moment: Everyone sits together for ten minutes before the restaurant opens.

Then:

Feeling: I would know the people I work beside, not just share a rota with them.

Then:

Desire: I could see myself in that team.

Or:

Feature: Career development.

Moment: A team member who joined reception is now being trained to take responsibility for events.

Feeling: People do not have to stay inside the box of their first job title.

Desire: There may be somewhere for me to go here.

That is more specific than saying "we invest in our people".

The same principle works throughout hospitality and leisure.

Show the spa team preparing before the first treatment.

Show what a briefing looks like at a hotel.

Let a cook explain what they learned in their first six months.

Follow an attraction employee through opening.

Ask someone at a holiday park why they return for another season.

The physical workplace gives you the setting.

The employee experience gives you the story.

Build an employer brand between vacancies

A common recruitment pattern looks like this:

The team is full.

Nothing is posted about working there.

Someone leaves.

A vacancy goes live.

Three recruitment posts appear.

The role is filled.

Employer content disappears again.

That is vacancy marketing.

It is not much of an employer brand.

A more useful content system works while the organisation is healthy, not only when it is under pressure.

Four types of content are particularly useful.

The work

Show the real job. Opening, prep, handovers, guest contact, problem-solving, the end of service, quiet moments and busy ones. The objective is clarity.

The people

Let employees carry part of the story. Not scripted testimonials about how "amazing" the company is, but specific answers: what surprised you when you started, what part of the job are you better at now, what kind of person enjoys working here, what is the hardest part of a busy day, why are you still here. Specificity creates more trust than praise.

The progress

Show what can change for someone who stays. Training, responsibility, internal moves, new skills, mentoring and genuine progression where it exists. Do not invent a career ladder that the business does not have.

The proof

Use real work, real employees and real experiences. If someone has been with the business for six years, that can carry more weight than a graphic saying "people come first." If the team returns each season, ask them why.

Employer branding works best when the evidence is already inside the organisation.

Give each channel a job

Candidates do not experience your employer brand on one page.

They build it from several signals.

A Reel. A TikTok. A LinkedIn post. A careers page. A Glassdoor or Indeed review. A conversation with someone who already works there. The website. The actual interview.

Research comparing hotel employer brands across websites and social media found considerable room for stronger and more consistent employer-brand communication across channels. More recent research into hotel corporate and career sites also shows how employers use different types of employee value propositions to communicate what employment offers beyond the vacancy itself.

That does not mean every channel should repeat the same content.

Instagram might help someone see the workplace.

TikTok may be useful for personality, people and less polished moments.

LinkedIn may carry more weight for experienced hires, management positions and professional development.

The careers or vacancy page then handles the practical questions.

Hours. Responsibilities. Pay where disclosed. Location. Shift patterns. Benefits. Application process.

The job of social content is not to squeeze the entire vacancy into a video.

It is to make the person on the other side interested enough, informed enough and trusting enough to continue.

That is the same principle behind our broader social media marketing strategy, where different content serves different stages of Discover → Feel → Trust → Consider → Act.

Employer branding simply applies the journey to a different person.

Better employer branding needs better signals than views

A recruitment video can reach 100,000 people and still do very little for the business.

A smaller piece of content might reach the exact chefs, hosts, therapists or managers you actually want.

So employer branding should not be judged by reach alone.

At the top of the journey, reach and video retention can show whether relevant people are discovering the workplace.

Then profile visits, careers-page clicks, saves, shares and meaningful questions can show deeper interest.

Closer to the decision, you can look at:

applications started, applications completed, qualified applicants, interviews and hires.

And after hiring, there is an even more important question.

Did the employment experience match what the candidate thought they were joining?

That matters because employer branding does not stop when someone signs a contract.

The internal experience is what eventually proves or disproves the external story.

Hospitality businesses therefore have something many employers would like to have.

A workplace full of visible moments.

People working together. Skills being learned. Pressure being handled. Guests being helped. Small routines that reveal culture.

You do not always need a bigger recruitment claim.

Sometimes you need to turn the camera around.

Not towards another room, plate, treatment or attraction.

Towards the people who make the experience possible.

Then show the right candidate what being one of them might actually feel like.
